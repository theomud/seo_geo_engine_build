Prompt Engineering

The 9-element system that turns a vague AI request into a 70%+-usable first draft. The prompt is not a question — it is a creative brief.

Why this skill exists

Most people use AI like a search engine: a vague question, a generic answer, every time. Prompt Engineering treats the model like a skilled team member who needs a brief — who they are, who they're writing for, what to produce, with what facts, under what constraints, to what standard. Do that and the first draft is 70%+ usable. It is the input multiplier for every content skill downstream.

The shift: stop asking "write a page about pet relocation to Dubai" (mush) and start briefing all 9 elements (a usable draft of a real page).

The 9 elements

Every strong prompt contains all nine. Each example below is from the Dubai proof niche.

1 · Context — the situation. "Dubai pet relocation, a maximum-risk regulated market; customers arrive afraid."
2 · Role — who the AI is. "A relocation specialist who has cleared pets through MOCCAE."
3 · Objective — exactly what to produce. "A 700-word Fear Resolution page on airport confiscation."
4 · Audience — the reader's state. "An owner who read their dog could be taken at the airport."
5 · Inputs — the facts to use. Verified C-019 permit online · C-010 90-day validity · C-003 500 AED release
6 · Constraints — avoid/include. "Open with the fear in the owner's words; hedge C-001 (titer cost); no 'Get a Quote'."
7 · Examples — show good output. "The 5-layer structure; embed the MOCCAE screenshot as proof."
8 · Output Format — how to structure it. "The 5 layers, markdown, each fact tagged with its C-ID."
9 · Quality Criteria — how it's judged. "Every claim cited or hedged; passes the Hormozi name-on-it test."
The two everyone skips — Constraints and Quality Criteria — are the two that decide whether the draft is usable.

The Minimum Viable Prompt

For quick tasks, the floor is four elements: Context + Role + Objective + Output Format. Never fewer.

"Dubai pet relocation, maximum-risk niche (context). You are a relocation specialist (role). Write 3 FAQ answers on the summer heat embargo, hedging anything MOCCAE doesn't publish (objective). Markdown Q&A (format)."
In the proof run this minimum-viable prompt produced usable summer-embargo FAQ answers — the embargo dates correctly hedged ("not published by MOCCAE", an Unverifiable input) rather than asserted.

The revision loop

First prompts rarely produce final output. Plan for 2–3 rounds, and always say what to change AND what to keep.

"Good start. Revise: (1) hedge the 700–1,300 AED line — it reads as official; (2) move the Etihad $399 fact up and name the $1,500 discrepancy. KEEP: the opening fear sentence and the MOCCAE-silence paragraph."
Saying only what's wrong lets the model fix the problem while breaking what was working.

The 70%-usable test

"Usable" is measured, not felt: count the edits to publish-ready.

70%+ usable = only light edits (tighten, reorder, one word). Ship after the revision loop.
Below 70% = structural rewrites or wrong/missing facts → the prompt is the problem. Fix an element (usually Constraints or Quality Criteria) and regenerate; don't hand-rewrite the output and call the prompt good.

Hard rule for a regulated niche: no Unverifiable claim may be asserted as fact — it is hedged or it is blocked.

Measured on the run: the titer-cost first draft came in ~70% usable; the single structural fix was adding the C-001 hedge to Constraints — after that, only light edits remained.

Real surprise from the proof run: the first titer-cost draft asserted "the titer test costs 700 AED" as fact. The miss was in the prompt, not the output — Constraints lacked the hedge rule for the Unverifiable C-001. We added the hedge clause and regenerated; we did not hand-edit the draft. That is the discipline: below-70% means fix the brief, not the page.
