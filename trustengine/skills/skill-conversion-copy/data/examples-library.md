# Classic Copy Breakdowns — Examples Library

Four historically documented examples, each dissected for the principle it proves and the framework it demonstrates. No screenshots — these are textual records of publicly documented campaigns.

---

## 1. Ogilvy — Rolls-Royce (1958)

### The headline

> "At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock."

**Primary public source.** David Ogilvy, *Confessions of an Advertising Man* (1963), Chapter 6; also reproduced in *Ogilvy on Advertising* (1983). The campaign ran in US newspapers and magazines, 1958. Widely reproduced in advertising history texts; the exact wording is consistent across Ogilvy's own accounts.

### What makes it work

The headline does not say "quality," "prestige," or "refinement." It gives a single, specific, verifiable sensory fact — one a reader could check. The reader does the inferencing. Because the claim is precise and checkable, it earns trust faster than any adjective could.

**Timeless principle.** Proof Over Promise (P-03 in the MFP library): one concrete detail outweighs any number of superlatives. Ogilvy's own gloss: *"I could have said 'This is a very quiet car' — but that would not have been as persuasive."*

**Framework demonstrated.** F-20 — Ogilvy's AICPBSAWN: the headline delivers Credibility and Proof before it delivers a Benefit. The reader reaches the benefit (luxury, silence) through the fact, not through assertion. This is also a clean instance of F-18 (AIDA): Attention is seized by the cognitive surprise of the electric-clock detail; Interest is held because the reader immediately wants to know what the rest of the car is like.

## 2. Avis — "We Try Harder" (1962)

### The copy (opening of the campaign ad)

> "Avis is only No. 2 in rent a cars. So why go with us? We try harder. (When you're not the biggest, you have to.)" [verify source — reproduced in Doyle Dane Bernbach agency records and numerous advertising history texts; the headline is consistent, the bracketed subline wording varies slightly across reproductions]

**Primary public source.** Campaign created by Doyle Dane Bernbach (DDB), 1962. Documented in *Bill Bernbach Said...* (DDB internal publication, 1989) and extensively in Luke Sullivan, *Hey Whipple, Squeeze This* (1st ed. 1998), pp. 54–56. The campaign ran for over a decade and is cited in most major advertising history curricula.

### What makes it work

The ad leads with an admission of weakness — the exact claim a competitor would weaponise. By saying it first, Avis owns the frame. The admission converts into a credibility signal (they're honest enough to say the hard thing) and a promise (because of their position, they are motivated to try harder). The reader's natural scepticism is disarmed before it forms.

**Timeless principle.** Pre-empting the objection: surface the weakness the reader is already thinking, then reframe it as a reason to buy. This is the structural move that makes the copy credible rather than defensive. It is also an instance of Cialdini's Liking principle (M-21): the brand makes itself relatable by admitting fallibility.

**Framework demonstrated.** F-19 — PAS (Problem · Agitate · Solution): the ad states the problem (we are second), agitates it minimally (why go with us?), and delivers the solution (we try harder) in the same sentence — not in a later paragraph. This is the "resolution in the same breath" discipline that P-04 (Fear-Acknowledging Not Fear-Exploiting) codifies. The campaign also demonstrates F-18 (AIDA) at the macro level: Attention via the counterintuitive admission, Action via the implied instruction to choose Avis.

## 3. Apple — "1,000 songs in your pocket" (2001)

### The line

> "1,000 songs in your pocket."

**Primary public source.** Steve Jobs keynote, Macworld Expo, San Francisco, 9 January 2001 (the iPod introduction). Transcript and video archived publicly; the line is quoted verbatim in Walter Isaacson, *Steve Jobs* (2011), Ch. 28, and in Apple's contemporaneous press materials. The exact line is consistent across all authoritative records.

### What makes it work

Apple's engineers described the iPod as a "5GB MP3 player." Jobs translated a technical specification (storage capacity) into a human outcome (how many songs I can carry) and then compressed it to a pocket-sized physical metaphor. The listener has instant, visceral understanding. There is no adjective in the line — only a number and a preposition.

**Timeless principle.** Benefits over features: state what the product does for the user, not what it is. More precisely, translate the technical fact into the experiential outcome the user actually cares about, then anchor it in a physical image. The number (1,000) provides the same kind of checkable specificity that Ogilvy's electric clock provides — it is falsifiable, which makes it credible.

**Framework demonstrated.** F-18 — AIDA: the line functions as the Attention and Desire steps simultaneously — the reader's imagination supplies the desire (that is a lot of music; I want that in my pocket). M-21 — Cialdini's principles: the "pocket" metaphor triggers a near-physical sense of possession before purchase (Commitment and Consistency — the reader has already imagined owning it). The line also demonstrates F-19 (PAS) in compressed form: the implied Problem (carrying a Discman and 20 CDs everywhere) is resolved in five words without being stated.

## 4. Eugene Schwartz / Classic Direct Response — Breakthrough Advertising (c. 1960s)

### The framing principle (not a single ad — a documented technique)

Eugene Schwartz did not sell with benefit claims. His documented technique, set out in *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966), was to identify the **most dominant desire** the prospect already holds and connect the product to that desire rather than educating the prospect into wanting something new.

**Representative example (reconstructed from Schwartz's own published analysis — [verify source for exact ad copy]):**

> "Now you can end joint pain in 7 days — without drugs, without surgery, and without the side effects that have kept you suffering."

This pattern — a specific outcome promise, a specific timeframe, and a triple "without" that pre-empts the standard objections — appears across Schwartz's documented work and is reproduced in his own published breakdowns in *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966, republished 2004 by Boardroom Inc.). The exact wording of individual ads is not always reliably reproduced in secondary sources; [verify source] for any specific headline attributed to Schwartz beyond what appears in the 1966 text itself.

**Primary public source.** Eugene Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966; Boardroom Inc. reprint 2004). Chapter 3 ("The Concept of the Market") and Chapter 6 ("Creating the Strongest Claim") are the specific sections where the dominant-desire framework is most explicitly laid out.

### What makes it work

The copy does not create desire — it channels desire that already exists. Schwartz's documented principle: *"Copy cannot create desire for a product. It can only take the hopes, dreams, fears and desires that already exist in the hearts of millions of people, and focus those already existing desires onto a particular product."* [This quotation is widely attributed to *Breakthrough Advertising*; verify page reference against the 2004 reprint for exactness.]

The "without" structure (without drugs / without surgery / without side effects) performs a second function: it pre-empts the three most predictable objections before the reader has formed them consciously, removing friction in the same sentence that builds desire.

**Timeless principle.** Meet desire where it already lives; do not create it. Pre-empt objections inside the promise rather than in a separate "but wait" passage. This is the direct-response version of the Avis move: the likely objection is absorbed into the claim itself.

**Framework demonstrated.** M-20 — Protection Motivation Theory: the prospect's existing fear (ongoing pain, failed treatments) is acknowledged (severity/susceptibility already present); the copy provides response efficacy (it works in 7 days) and self-efficacy (you can do this without drugs/surgery). This is structurally identical to the fear-acknowledging opening in P-04: fear is stated, then resolved credibly, in the same passage. F-19 — PAS: Problem (pain), Agitate (implied — the drugs/surgery you have already tried), Solution (this product, with a timeframe). The "without" clauses are the agitation compressed into the solution line, which prevents the reader from dwelling in unresolved fear.
