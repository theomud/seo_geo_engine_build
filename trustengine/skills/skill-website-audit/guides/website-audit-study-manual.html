Website Audit

Score any website on all 13 Trust Engine skills — 130 points, every score backed by evidence quoted from the live site, ending in a RICE-ranked action plan a business owner can act on.

Why this skill

Ask the usual tools "is my website any good?" and they answer the wrong question. Lighthouse grades page speed. An SEO crawler counts meta tags. A heatmap shows clicks. None answers the question that decides whether the site makes money: does a frightened, high-stakes visitor trust this enough to act — and if not, what is the single highest-leverage thing to fix first?

"Every website says something different — I don't know who to trust." — the customer a trust audit is built to win back.

This skill turns the entire 13-skill Trust Engine into one scoring instrument: 13 dimensions, each scored /10 with evidence quoted from the live site, totalling /130 — then a RICE-ranked plan and a ready-to-execute content brief for the top gap. It's the F-11 audit discipline pointed outward at a real site, with Trust at the centre M-24 P-22.

Score by evidence not opinion

A trust audit is not a checklist a script can tick — it's evidenced judgement. Three rules keep it rigorous instead of opinion P-03 P-07:

Opinion
"Dimension 1: 3/10 — the homepage could name its fears better." (a vibe; the owner can't act on it)
Evidence
"Dimension 1: 3/10 — the homepage opens 'Animal Care. Animal Relocations. By Animal People' and never names the confiscation fear." (a finding, with a quote)

1. Calibrate the proof bar first (M-04). 2. Every score carries a quote from the live site (P-03). 3. Rank the fixes by consequence, not ease (M-27). No quote → no score. If you didn't see it on the page, it doesn't count.

The proof bar

Calibrate the risk level M-04 before scoring — it sets the bar every claim must clear. The same uncited fee is a footnote on a low-risk site and a scored failure on a maximum-risk one.

DKC is maximum-risk (YMYL): wrong information means a pet denied entry P-02. So every claim DKC makes about permits, vaccinations, or fees must be cited to an official source — or it loses points on Dimension 3. Score against the right bar, or the whole audit is miscalibrated.

The RICE action plan

Thirteen dimensions surface more gaps than anyone can fix at once. RICE M-27 turns them into one ordered queue, so the highest-leverage fix is unambiguous F-05 — the report ends in actions, not observations.

RICE = (Reach × Impact × Confidence) ÷ Effort        (top 3 of DKC's plan)
1  Publish the airport-confiscation page   (9×3×0.9)/2 = 12.15   ← single most important
2  Put cost ranges on the page (not a PDF) (7×2×0.8)/1 = 11.2
3  Add "last verified" dates to reg facts  (6×2×0.9)/1 = 10.8

The #1 action ships with a complete 9-element content brief — Inputs filled with the real audit data: the verbatim fear quote, and the verified facts (90-day permit C-010, 500 AED release fee C-003, 21-day rabies rule C-007, online permit C-019, honest titer hedge C-001). It's runnable as-is.

Keeping it honest

An audit fails in the way hardest to catch: it looks authoritative. Four defences keep it real:

  • Every score has a quote from the live site — re-open the page and confirm the quote is actually there. A quote that isn't on the site is fabricated evidence and voids the dimension.
  • The proof bar matches the risk level — a maximum-risk site held to "official source for every claim."
  • A second auditor re-scores blind and lands within ±1, or the scoring is opinion, not evidence P-07.
  • The executive summary passes the client-ready test — a business owner could act on it without the framework explained.

On the DKC run this discipline showed: the FAQ schema (Dimension 11) couldn't be confirmed from the fetch, so it was flagged needs Rich Results Test — not asserted. The audit never claims what it didn't see.
