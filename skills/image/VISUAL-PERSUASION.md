# Visual Persuasion — the image-psychology skill

*The evidence-tiered companion to the copywriting doctrine, for the picture half of the engine.
It governs WHY an image is chosen and HOW it's prompted — so images do the same psychological work
as the words, on the same standard of honesty.*

> **Confidence labels** (same as the knowledge base): **strong** (well-established) · **good**
> (sound, context matters) · **limited** (directional) · **weak** (illustrative only).
>
> **Standing caveat — read this.** Image-persuasion evidence is generally **weaker and more
> context-dependent than copywriting evidence**, and **none of our specific images have been tested**
> (no A/B, eye-tracking, or conversion data). So this is principled best practice, **not proof that a
> given image converts** — exactly the honesty we hold on SEO/GEO. Treat it as a hypothesis to test.

---

## 1. Picture-superiority effect — *use meaningful images, not decoration*
**Evidence (strong).** People recall pictures far better than words — roughly **twice as many** in
classic studies — because images are dual-coded (stored as both an image and a verbal label) while
words have a single path (Paivio, dual-coding theory, 1973; replicated — Memory & Cognition).
**Caveat:** the effect shrinks when words are made distinctive, and a picture isn't *always* better
(CMU memory-lab) — so the image must carry **meaning**, not just decorate.
**Application:** every hero must *say something* (the outcome, the process), never fill space.
**Prompt rule:** depict the specific meaningful moment (calm pet + IATA crate = "safe, handled").

## 2. Faces & gaze direction — *use a human face, point its gaze at the message*
**Evidence (good→strong; NN/G eyetracking).** People reliably look at **faces, especially eyes**, and
**follow the model's gaze**: in the classic baby-ad study, when the baby looks at the headline,
viewers' eyes follow to the headline too (NN/G eyetracking; face-direction attention, PMC).
**Application:** include a person; have them **look toward the pet / the key message / the CTA**, not
straight out. A reassured owner looking at their calm pet pulls the viewer's eye to the outcome.
**Prompt rule:** "owner gazing at their calm pet" > "pet alone"; direct the gaze toward the point.

## 3. Authenticity vs stock — *real beats model-stock, and builds trust*
**Evidence (good; NN/G "Photos as Web Content").** Users **scrutinise relevant, authentic photos**
(real people/products) and **ignore decorative stock**. Strikingly, **people who look like models draw
LESS attention than "normal" people** — a direct reason to avoid glossy stock. Authentic, current
imagery is also a **trust signal** (NN/G credibility research; CXL).
**Application:** original, real-feeling, relevant images of *this* situation; avoid polished
model-stock. (Our auditor already rewards original visuals and penalises stock — this is the why.)
**Prompt rule:** realistic ordinary people + real settings; "photorealistic, natural, not a stock-photo look."

## 4. Visual hierarchy — *make the outcome/CTA the most prominent thing*
**Evidence (strong; NN/G, Gestalt, Laws of UX).** The eye lands on the most visually dominant element
first; size, contrast and position direct attention. (This is design — David's build — but it shapes
what the hero must support.)
**Application:** the hero should make the **reassuring outcome** the focal point; downstream, the
single CTA should be the most prominent action.
**Prompt rule:** clear subject, shallow depth of field, uncluttered background → one clear focal point.

## 5. Colour & warmth — *mood, yes; universal meaning, no*
**Evidence (weak / contested).** There is **little support for universal colour meanings** ("orange
makes people buy"). **Colour-in-context theory**: effects depend on context, culture and learned
association — the same red means "sale", "danger", "hunger" or "passion" by context (Help Scout/Ciotti;
academic). Warm colours do raise arousal physiologically, but meaning is situational.
**Application:** use **warm, calm light for a reassuring mood** (fits a fear/trust market) — but do
**not** treat colour as a reliable persuasion lever or claim universal effects.
**Prompt rule:** "warm, soft, natural light" for reassurance; brand colour is a design choice, not a conversion guarantee.

## The image prompt checklist (derived from the above)
- [ ] **Meaningful** scene (shows the outcome/process), not decoration — §1
- [ ] **A person**, gaze pointed at the pet / key message — §2
- [ ] **Authentic, ordinary, real** (not model-stock); original, not generic — §3
- [ ] **One clear focal point**; uncluttered; shallow depth of field — §4
- [ ] **Warm, soft, natural light** for reassurance (mood, not a colour claim) — §5
- [ ] **Photorealistic · no text · no logos · no lettering** (FLUX stamps fake text — exclude it)
- [ ] **3:2 hero crop**; low-res by default (full-res once approved)
- [ ] **Descriptive alt text** stored in the content JSON (accessibility + an SEO/GEO signal)
- [ ] **No fear-mongering / dark patterns** — name fears in words, resolve them in the image

## Pet-relocation application
Sell the **relief**, not the service: a calm, healthy pet with a caring owner/handler beside a labelled
IATA crate in warm, bright light — the happy ending the anxious owner is afraid they won't get. The
copy names the fear ("will my pet suffer in cargo?"); the image answers it visually with safety, care
and competence. Process shots (a vet scanning a microchip) build trust by showing we know the steps.

## What we're sure of vs not
- **Strong / can rely on:** picture-superiority (meaningful images), authenticity-over-stock, visual hierarchy.
- **Good, context matters:** faces + gaze direction.
- **Weak / don't over-claim:** colour psychology (context-dependent, not universal).
- **Unproven for OUR images:** we have not tested any of our images — no eye-tracking, A/B or conversion
  data. The rationale is sound; "does this image convert" is a hypothesis until measured.
