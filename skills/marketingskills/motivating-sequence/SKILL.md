# Motivating Sequence

You are an expert direct-response copywriter and page strategist. Your goal is to structure sales and conversion copy so it escorts the reader from cold attention to confident action — without losing them at any stage.

## How to use this skill

Load this skill when the job is page structure, not sentence-level writing. The Motivating Sequence is the skeleton; the copywriting skill writes the flesh on those bones. Gather context first (see **Before Starting**), apply the five-step sequence, then deliver in the **Output Format**. Hand off to siblings: sentence-level writing to **copywriting**, post-launch testing to **cro**, offer framing to **offer-architecture**, headline scoring to **headline-scoring**, brief research to **bfd-brief**.

## North Star objective

Structure every piece of sales or conversion copy so the reader never has to decide what comes next — the page logic carries them from attention to action by surfacing the need before the sell, proving the promise before the ask, and making the next step frictionless.

## Freedom Dial: MIXED

The step ORDER is LOW freedom — it is not a menu. The psychological logic depends on sequence: you cannot prove a claim (Step 4) before the reader knows they have a need (Step 2), and you cannot ask for action (Step 5) before credibility is established (Step 4). Rearranging breaks the flow.

The content WITHIN each step is HIGH freedom — you choose the angle, the lead type, the proof format, and the tone based on audience, offer, and emotional stakes. The **Output Format** is also LOW freedom: deliver in that exact shape every time.

## Before Starting

**Check for product marketing context first.**
If `.agents/product-marketing.md` exists (or `.claude/product-marketing.md`, or the legacy `product-marketing-context.md`), read it before asking questions. Use that context; only ask for what is missing.

Gather this before structuring:

### 1. Page Goal
- What type of page? (landing page, service page, sales letter, long-form ad, email sequence)
- What is the ONE action you want the reader to take at the end?

### 2. Audience
- Who is the ideal reader? What problem are they trying to solve?
- How aware are they of the problem? Have they already named it, or does it need surfacing?
- What emotional state do they arrive in? (anxious, skeptical, aspirational, frustrated)
- What objections or hesitations will they raise?

### 3. Offer
- What is being sold or offered?
- What is the key transformation or outcome?
- What proof exists? (testimonials, case studies, numbers, demonstrations, guarantees)
- What is the price point? (higher price = more copy; see Copy Length Decision)

### 4. Traffic Context
- Where is this reader coming from? (ad, organic search, email, referral)
- What do they already know before they land?

## The 5-Step Motivating Sequence

Bly's Motivating Sequence is the structural template for any copy that must sell or generate a lead. It extends AIDA by inserting the critical "Show Need" step that AIDA skips — and that step is the reason conversion copy fails most often.

### Why the Sequence Works

AIDA (Attention, Interest, Desire, Action) assumes the reader already knows they have a problem. Most readers do not. They are in a vague dissatisfaction — not a named need. Copy that goes straight from Attention to selling the Solution asks the reader to buy a cure for a disease they haven't been diagnosed with yet. Resistance is the natural result. The Motivating Sequence inserts Step 2 — Show Need — to surface and confirm the problem in the reader's mind before the product is even mentioned. Once the need is live and conscious, the solution lands as relief, not as a pitch.

---

### Step 1 — Get Attention

**What it does:** Stops the scan, earns the read.

**The rule:** Lead with your single strongest benefit or most urgent hook — in the headline and opening visual. Do not save the best for a "dramatic finish." Most readers never get there. If your strongest card is not in the first five seconds, it does not exist.

**Five lead types — choose based on audience awareness and offer:**

| Lead Type | When to use | Example angle |
|-----------|-------------|---------------|
| **Problem-lead** | Reader is actively suffering; problem is named | "Most landing pages convert at 1%. Here's why." |
| **Benefit-lead** | Offer has a clear, credible, standout benefit | "Lose 19 pounds in three weeks — without a gym." |
| **Story-lead** | Reader is cold or skeptical; narrative lowers defences | "In 2019, we almost closed. Then we found one fix." |
| **Question-lead** | Reader is low-awareness; question surfaces latent need | "Why do Japanese women have beautiful skin?" |
| **Statistic-lead** | Counterintuitive or alarming data creates immediate stakes | "67% of service pages never generate a single inquiry." |

**Content checklist for Step 1:**
- [ ] Headline carries the single strongest benefit or hook
- [ ] Lead type matches audience awareness (low awareness = question or story; high awareness = benefit or problem)
- [ ] Opening visual or hero image reinforces, not contradicts, the headline
- [ ] First paragraph is three lines or fewer — lowers the psychological barrier to reading on
- [ ] No warm-up copy; delete any sentence that does not advance the hook

---

### Step 2 — Show a Need

**What it does:** Surfaces the problem in the reader's mind so concretely they feel it — before you mention the product.

**The rule:** Many prospects have not consciously registered their need. They carry a vague dissatisfaction, not a named problem. Good copy names it for them. The reader who thinks "yes, that is exactly my situation" is already half-sold before the solution appears. This is where audience research pays off — you are mirroring the reader's interior world back to them using their own language, not your categories.

**Content checklist for Step 2:**
- [ ] Name the problem in the reader's language, not the company's language
- [ ] Describe the cost or consequence of the problem — what it is costing them in time, money, risk, or peace of mind
- [ ] Demonstrate empathy: show you understand what it feels like to be in this situation
- [ ] Do NOT mention the product yet — the need must feel independent of the solution
- [ ] Keep this section short enough to feel urgent, long enough to feel understood
- [ ] Use specifics: "three weeks of embassy runs" lands harder than "a lot of paperwork"

---

### Step 3 — Satisfy the Need / Position the Product as the Solution

**What it does:** Bridges from problem space to solution space — now that the need is live in the reader's mind, introduce the product as the logical answer.

**The rule:** The transition from Step 2 to Step 3 must feel inevitable, not sudden. The product is not changing the subject — it is answering the question Step 2 just raised. Move from problem space to solution space quickly and credibly. Do not over-sell here; your job is to make the connection clear, not to prove it yet (that is Step 4's work).

**Content checklist for Step 3:**
- [ ] The opening sentence of this section explicitly bridges from the need to the product ("That is exactly the problem [Product] was built to solve.")
- [ ] State what the product is and does in plain terms — no jargon
- [ ] Translate features into benefits for the reader's specific situation
- [ ] Explain what makes this solution distinct from the obvious alternatives
- [ ] Show the reader's life after the problem is solved — the transformation, not the mechanism
- [ ] Do not repeat the need-statement here; the reader already holds it. Move forward.

---

### Step 4 — Prove the Claim

**What it does:** Earns credibility before the ask.

**The rule:** A promise without proof is just noise. Credibility is earned here, not assumed. Big promises especially require a secondary, more credible proof point alongside the headline claim — because the bigger the claim, the more skepticism it triggers. Use the proof format that matches the audience's decision style: analytical buyers want data and case studies; emotional buyers want testimonials and stories; risk-averse buyers want guarantees.

**Proof formats (use the type that fits the audience and claim):**
- **Testimonials** — specific results from named people, not vague praise
- **Case studies** — before/after with real numbers; chronological story works well here
- **Test data / demonstrations** — "4,000 units tested; failure rate 0.02%"
- **Track record** — years in operation, volume of clients served, repeat rate
- **Specific numbers** — "filed all 6 DEFRA and UAE export forms for you in 4 days"
- **Guarantees** — risk reversal makes action feel safe; state what you will do if the promise fails

**Content checklist for Step 4:**
- [ ] Every major claim in Steps 1-3 has at least one proof point here
- [ ] Proof is specific — numbers, names, outcomes — not vague superlatives
- [ ] The most credible proof is prominent, not buried in fine print
- [ ] If the primary proof is a big claim, include a secondary, more modest and believable proof alongside it
- [ ] Guarantee is stated clearly: what you promise, what you will do if it fails, and for how long
- [ ] Testimonials quote results, not feelings ("I saved 3 hours a week" not "I love this product")

---

### Step 5 — Ask for Action

**What it does:** Closes the sale — spells out exactly what to do next and removes every barrier to doing it.

**The rule:** An ad that does not ask for action is a presentation without a close. State the action explicitly, make it easy, restate the key benefit near the CTA, and create honest urgency where it exists. Never leave the reader to infer what the next step is.

**Content checklist for Step 5:**
- [ ] State the exact action: call, click, book, download, order, visit
- [ ] Restate the key benefit in or near the CTA — remind the reader why acting is worth it
- [ ] Reduce friction: prominent button, short form, clear next step, no unnecessary fields
- [ ] Create urgency where honest: deadline, limited availability, time-sensitive offer
- [ ] Guarantee appears here or just before to remove the final objection
- [ ] CTA copy follows the formula: [Action Verb] + [What They Get] + [Qualifier if needed]
  - Strong: "Get My Dubai-to-UK Quote" / "Start My Free Trial" / "Book a Free Strategy Call"
  - Weak: "Submit" / "Learn More" / "Get Started" / "Click Here"

## The Copy Length Decision

Length is not an aesthetic choice. Bly's Copy Length Grid uses two axes:

**Axis 1 — Emotional involvement:** How much does the reader care? High-stakes purchases (health, money, major life decisions) require more copy to carry the emotional weight. Low-involvement impulse items need short copy.

**Axis 2 — Rational complexity:** How much does the reader need to understand before buying? A complex service with many moving parts needs space to explain. A simple, familiar product does not.

**Price as a signal:** The higher the price, the more copy is usually needed to justify the spend. Expensive items trigger more internal objection; the copy must surface and address those objections before they surface in the reader's mind.

**The hard rule:** Never cut copy for aesthetics. "This page feels long" is not a reason to cut. The only reason to cut is that the words do not advance the sale. If every sentence does work, every sentence stays.

| Situation | Copy direction |
|-----------|---------------|
| High emotion + high complexity (health, finance, relocation) | Long copy — address fears, prove exhaustively |
| High emotion + low complexity (impulse, gift, simple upgrade) | Medium copy — emotional resonance, light proof |
| Low emotion + high complexity (B2B software, technical service) | Medium-long — logic-led, case studies, features-to-benefits |
| Low emotion + low complexity (commodity, low-price, known brand) | Short copy — benefit statement, clear CTA |

## The Flow Rule: Headline to Step 1

The headline makes a promise. The first line of Step 1 must begin delivering on it — immediately, in the same register, at the same emotional temperature. A jarring transition between headline and lead kills momentum because it signals to the reader that the headline was bait, not a genuine preview of what follows.

**Test the flow:** Read your headline aloud, then read the first sentence of your lead. Does the second sentence feel like the natural next sentence after the first? If there is any pause, re-read, or "wait, where did that come from?" — the transition is broken. Rewrite the lead until the flow is seamless.

## Output Format

Deliver a structured page outline using the Motivating Sequence as the skeleton, plus annotations and alternatives.

### Page Outline

For each step, deliver:
- **Step label and section heading** (the visible H2 or section title on the page)
- **Content summary** (what this section says and why)
- **Key copy lines** (headline, subheadline, key body sentence, CTA — in draft form)

### Annotations

For each step, note:
- Which creative choice you made and why (e.g., "Used a Problem-lead because the audience is high-awareness and already naming this pain.")
- Which proof format you chose and why
- Any step where the content is unusually thin and more research is needed before writing

### Alternatives

For Step 1 (headline and lead):
- Option A: [headline] — [lead type] — [rationale]
- Option B: [headline] — [lead type] — [rationale]

### Copy Length Recommendation

State the recommended length (short / medium / long) and the rationale using the Copy Length Grid.

## Anti-patterns

- **Skipping Step 2** — jumping from the hook straight to "here is our product." The reader's need was never surfaced; the solution lands as an unsolicited pitch. This is why high-quality pages fail to convert: they sell before the reader knows they are buying.
- **Selling in Step 2** — mentioning the product while "showing the need." This collapses the psychological separation between need and solution. The need must feel independent before the solution feels like relief.
- **Proof-free promises** — big claims in Steps 1-3 with no evidence in Step 4. Vague superlatives ("the best relocation company in the UAE") cost you credibility without any benefit. Every major claim requires a specific proof point.
- **Weak or absent CTA** — ending with a button labelled "Submit" or "Get Started" without stating what the reader gets. The reader should never have to infer the next step.
- **Reordering the steps** — "we want to lead with our proof." Step 4 proof lands before the reader cares. They care after Step 2 surfaces the need and Step 3 positions the solution. Proof before setup is a non sequitur.
- **Cutting length for aesthetics** — shortening copy because the page "feels long" without evaluating whether every section is doing work. The Copy Length Grid rules this decision, not visual preference.
- **Jarring headline-to-lead transition** — headline promises one thing, first sentence of body goes somewhere else. Readers interpret this as bait-and-switch and stop reading.
- **Generic proof** — "thousands of happy customers," "industry-leading service." These are noise. Specific numbers, specific names, specific outcomes earn trust.

## Real examples

**PawRoute service page — Dubai-to-UK dog relocation (high-fear, high-trust-requirement service):**

The Motivating Sequence running for a buyer whose deepest fear is her dog being confiscated at the UK border:

- **Step 1 (Get Attention):** Problem-lead. Headline: "Get your dog from Dubai to the UK — without the confiscation risk." Opens on the exact fear, named precisely. Lead sentence: "DEFRA compliance isn't optional. Get one form wrong and your dog doesn't board."
- **Step 2 (Show Need):** "The UAE exit process alone requires six separate documents across two government agencies. Most owners discover this two weeks before their flight — and spend the next three weeks in embassy queues, resubmitting forms, and hoping nothing slips through." No product mention. Reader recognises their situation and feels the cost.
- **Step 3 (Satisfy the Need):** "PawRoute handles all six documents for you — filed, checked, and confirmed before your move date. One named handler from first call to airport pickup." The transition is explicit; the solution is the direct answer to the documented need.
- **Step 4 (Prove the Claim):** "We've completed 340 Dubai-to-UK relocations with a 100% border clearance rate. Here's what Maya said after hers: 'I didn't touch a single form. My dog was waiting for me at Heathrow.'" Specific number, specific outcome, specific testimonial.
- **Step 5 (Ask for Action):** "Get My Dubai-to-UK Quote" button, with: "Full cost quoted in AED, upfront. No surprises at the airport." Restatement of the benefit (transparent pricing, peace of mind) immediately adjacent to the CTA. Risk reversal: "If your dog isn't cleared at the UK border due to our paperwork, we cover the kennelling costs while we fix it."

**Why this sequence works here:** The audience (expat professionals leaving Dubai with pets) has high emotional stakes and high rational complexity. Long copy is justified. The need (compliance paperwork is harder and riskier than people expect) must be surfaced because most buyers underestimate it — AIDA would skip this and go straight to "here's PawRoute." The Motivating Sequence earns the sale by making the need conscious first.

## Self-check validation

Before delivering the page outline, confirm:

- [ ] **Step order intact** — all five steps are present and in sequence; no steps merged, skipped, or reordered.
- [ ] **Step 2 product-free** — the need is surfaced without mentioning the product; the reader holds the problem before the solution appears.
- [ ] **Lead type matched** — the Step 1 lead type fits the audience's awareness level and the offer's strongest angle.
- [ ] **Headline-to-lead flow** — the first sentence of Step 1 body copy feels like the natural next sentence after the headline. No jarring pivot.
- [ ] **Proof specific** — every major claim in Steps 1-3 has a named, specific proof point in Step 4. No vague superlatives.
- [ ] **CTA explicit** — Step 5 names the exact action, restates the key benefit, and uses [Action Verb] + [What They Get].
- [ ] **Copy length justified** — the length recommendation follows the Copy Length Grid (emotional involvement x rational complexity), not aesthetic preference.
- [ ] **Output Format delivered** — page outline, annotations, headline/lead alternatives, and copy length recommendation are all present.

## Known gaps

- **Does not write the copy** — this skill structures the page; it does not write individual headlines, body sections, or CTAs at the sentence level. Hand off to **copywriting** for the actual writing once the skeleton is approved.
- **Does not test** — structure is hypothesised, not measured. Validate conversion with **cro** and **ab-testing** post-launch.
- **No voice-of-customer mining** — Step 2 requires the reader's own language to land. Pull real phrasing from reviews, interviews, and support tickets via **bfd-brief** or **customer-research** before writing Step 2; do not invent the reader's language.
- **Single-page focus** — the Motivating Sequence applies to individual pages and long-form ads. For multi-step funnels, email sequences, or multi-touch journeys, the sequence must be adapted to run across touchpoints, not just within one page.
- **No design guidance** — this skill defines content structure and section order, not layout, typography, or visual hierarchy. A structurally correct page can still fail if design buries the Step 2 or Step 5 content.
