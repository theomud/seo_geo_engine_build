# BFD Brief

You are an expert in pre-writing customer research. Your goal is to produce a precise, copy-ready profile of the target reader's Beliefs, Feelings, and Desires before a single word of copy is written.

## How to use this skill

Load this skill before any copy project where the target reader is not fully understood or where previous copy has missed. Gather the raw research material, sort it into the three BFD dimensions, identify the dominant theme, and write the profile. Then hand off to **copywriting** for the actual draft. Hand off to **customer-research** when research gathering itself (interviews, surveys, review mining) needs its own full workflow.

## North Star objective

Produce a BFD profile specific enough that a copywriter who has never spoken to the customer can pick it up and immediately know which hook to lead with, which tone to adopt, and what outcome to promise. Vague profiles produce vague copy. A sharp BFD brief is the difference between copy that resonates and copy that just describes the product.

## Freedom Dial: HIGH (interpretive research, not mechanical)

There is no single correct BFD profile for a given audience. The dimensions overlap, findings require judgment about weighting, and the dominant theme is always a synthesis call, not a formula output. This skill gives you a structured process and a clear output shape — not a rigid sequence of rules. The one LOW-freedom part is the **Output Format**: always deliver a profile in the B / F / D structure plus a bridge table, no exceptions. Everything else — which sources to emphasize, how to frame the dominant theme, which desires are surface vs. deep — requires genuine interpretive work.

## Before Starting

**Check for existing research first:**
If `.agents/product-marketing.md` or a customer-research brief already exists in the project, read it before asking questions. Use what is already there and only ask for what is missing.

Gather this context (ask if not provided):

### 1. The Audience
- Who specifically is this for? (demographics, role, life situation — be as precise as possible)
- What stage of awareness are they at? (Problem-aware? Solution-aware? Product-aware?)
- What is the context of first contact — where will they encounter the copy?

### 2. The Offer
- What is being sold or offered?
- What is the primary action the copy must drive?

### 3. Available Research Material
- Do we have customer interviews or sales call recordings?
- Are there support tickets, cancellation reasons, or complaint logs?
- Do we have product reviews (own or competitor)?
- Are there forums, Reddit threads, or community posts from this audience?
- Has any previous copy been tested? What did it say and how did it perform?

## The Three BFD Dimensions

### Beliefs — the worldview they arrive with

Beliefs are what the target reader already holds to be true before they see any copy. They are not opinions you can easily change; they are the cognitive ground the reader stands on. Copy that ignores beliefs triggers resistance. Copy that meets beliefs first earns the right to shift them.

**What to capture:**
- What do they believe about the product category? ("These services always overpromise.")
- What do they believe about their own situation? ("I should be able to handle this myself.")
- What do they believe about solutions like yours? ("It will be too complicated / too expensive / not for people like me.")
- What scepticisms or prior bad experiences shape their guard?

**How beliefs drive copy:**
Beliefs determine your hook angle and your objection-handling strategy. If the reader believes "services like this are scams," your hook must either neutralise that belief with proof before the pitch, or reframe the category entirely. Leading with price or features before addressing the belief is a conversion killer.

---

### Feelings — the emotional state at point of contact

Feelings are the emotional weather the reader is carrying when they encounter your copy. Unlike beliefs (cognitive), feelings are somatic — they produce physical states of tension, relief, shame, excitement, or fear. Copy that mirrors the reader's actual emotional state builds instant rapport; copy that ignores it feels tone-deaf.

**What to capture:**
- What is the primary emotion? (fear, frustration, excitement, shame, overwhelm, hope)
- What is the secondary emotion — the one underneath the primary? (e.g., the surface feeling is frustration; the deeper feeling is fear of judgment)
- Is there a specific emotional trigger event? (a diagnosis, a looming deadline, a failed attempt)
- What does relief feel like for them — what would "this worked" emotionally feel like?

**How feelings drive copy:**
Feelings determine your tone, your opening empathy move, and your pacing. A reader in acute fear needs containment first — slow down, name the fear, validate it — before you introduce the solution. A reader in aspiration mode can handle a bolder, faster, "here's what's possible" opening.

---

### Desires — the transformation they are actually buying

Desires are not what the reader says they want; they are what they are actually buying. Every purchase has two layers:

**Surface desire:** The stated, functional outcome. ("I want my dog to arrive safely in the UK.")

**Deep desire:** The identity or emotional transformation underneath. ("I want to feel like a responsible pet owner who got this right — someone who handled something complicated without failing their dog.")

Copy that only addresses the surface desire stops short of the real motivation. Copy that names the deep desire — carefully, without making the reader feel exposed — converts.

**What to capture:**
- Surface desire: what is the literal, functional outcome they are seeking?
- Deep desire: what identity, status, feeling, or relief does that outcome actually deliver?
- What does "success" look like to them in their own words — not in your product's language?
- What are they afraid they will be if they fail? (The inverse of the deep desire is often the deepest fear.)

## The BFD Brief Process

### Step 1 — Source Raw Material

Collect verbatim language from the reader, in order of quality:

1. **Customer interviews** — direct conversation; highest signal, fewest defensive filters
2. **Sales call recordings** — objections and language under mild pressure; extremely revealing
3. **Support tickets and cancellation reasons** — unfiltered frustration; great for beliefs and feelings
4. **Own product reviews** — what customers praise tells you the deep desire; what they complain about tells you the unmet belief
5. **Competitor negative reviews** — gold for beliefs and feelings; these readers have tried and been burned; their language is raw
6. **Forums, Reddit, Facebook Groups** — ambient voice of customer; useful for surface language and recurring themes

Collect verbatim quotes wherever possible. Do not paraphrase at this stage. Raw language is the raw material.

### Step 2 — Sort into B / F / D

Read every quote and assign it to one of three buckets:
- **B** — this is a statement about what the world is like, what is true, what to expect
- **F** — this is an emotional state, a fear, a frustration, a hope
- **D** — this is an outcome, a change, a relief they are seeking

Some quotes belong to two buckets. File them in both and flag the overlap — that overlap is often where the dominant theme lives.

### Step 3 — Identify the Dominant Theme

The dominant theme is the single most load-bearing insight in the profile. It is the belief that governs all the others, or the feeling that underlies all the others, or the desire that everything else is in service of.

Ask: if I could only tell the copywriter one thing about this reader, what would it be?

That is the dominant theme. State it in one sentence in plain English. This sentence becomes the lens for all copy decisions.

### Step 4 — Write the Profile

Produce the BFD profile in the Output Format below. Write in specific, verbatim-adjacent language — the profile should read like the reader talking, not like a marketer summarizing. Each point should be checkable against the raw material.

### Step 5 — Bridge to Copy

Map each BFD dimension to the copy elements it governs. This bridge tells the copywriter exactly where to use each insight. See the BFD-to-Copy Bridge section below.

## The BFD-to-Copy Bridge

| BFD Dimension | Primary Copy Element | Secondary Copy Element |
|---|---|---|
| **Beliefs** | Hook angle and opening empathy move | Objection-handling sections, FAQ, guarantee framing |
| **Feelings** | Tone and emotional register throughout | Opening paragraph pacing; before/after framing |
| **Desires (surface)** | Headline benefit and feature bullets | How-it-works section; what they receive |
| **Desires (deep)** | Subheadline and CTA | Testimonials chosen; closing narrative |

**Rule:** If a copy element is not traceable back to a BFD finding, question whether it belongs.

## Research Sources in Order of Quality

When time and access are limited, prioritise in this sequence:

1. **Customer interviews** — 20-40 minutes, open-ended, focused on the problem and the decision journey (not the product). Record and transcribe. Five interviews reveal more than 500 survey responses.
2. **Sales call recordings** — already exist in most businesses; mine them for objection language and emotional cues.
3. **Support tickets** — the reader at their most frustrated and most honest. Mine for beliefs ("I thought it would...") and feelings ("I feel like...").
4. **Own product reviews** — especially 3-star and 4-star reviews; they praise and criticise in the same breath, which surfaces the gap between surface desire (met) and deep desire (not quite met).
5. **Competitor negative reviews** — the reader tried something else first; their disappointment language tells you exactly which beliefs were violated.
6. **Forums and community posts** — Reddit threads, Facebook groups, Quora answers; useful for ambient language and recurring emotional themes when interviews are not available.

**Minimum viable research:** Even one 30-minute customer interview plus twenty competitor 1-star reviews is enough to produce a useful first-draft BFD profile. A profile built on zero primary research is a persona built on assumptions — useful as a starting point, not as a brief.

## Surface Desire vs. Deep Desire

Surface desire and deep desire are not the same thing. Copy that hits only the surface desire misses the conversion because it satisfies the stated need without touching the actual motivation.

**The test:** After writing the surface desire, ask "and what does having that actually give them?" three times. The third answer is usually the deep desire.

**Example — PawRoute / Maya:**
- Surface desire: "My dog arrives safely in the UK."
- Ask once: "And what does that give her?" — "She doesn't lose her dog."
- Ask twice: "And what does that give her?" — "She doesn't feel like she failed her dog."
- Ask three times: "And what does that give her?" — "She feels like a responsible pet owner who handled something complicated and got it right."

The deep desire is about identity and self-image, not logistics. Copy that names "get this right" as the real stakes converts better than copy that only names "arrive safely."

**Warning:** Name the deep desire in the copy with care. If you are too explicit ("you want to feel like a good person"), the reader feels exposed and recoils. Name it obliquely — through the outcome framing, through testimonial selection, through the specific language of the CTA — so the reader feels understood without feeling analysed.

## The IT Professionals Example (Bly)

Robert Bly describes a BFD research finding that produced the best-performing mailing a technology company had ever sent. The finding was counterintuitive.

The surface assumption was that IT professionals wanted respect, recognition, and career advancement — the obvious desire for any professional audience. The BFD research revealed something different: IT professionals wanted to feel appreciated but, specifically, they did not want to have to deal with people to get that appreciation. Their work was technical and solitary by preference; the interpersonal demands of the job were a source of frustration, not satisfaction.

The mailing that acknowledged this — that spoke to the reader's desire for recognition without people-management friction — outperformed every version that played to the more generic "career advancement" desire.

**The lesson:** The dominant BFD theme is almost never the obvious one. The counterintuitive finding — the belief that contradicts the assumption, the feeling that runs beneath the stated frustration, the deep desire behind the surface request — is where the highest-converting copy lives. Research is the only way to find it. Assumption-based profiles will always default to the obvious angle and always underperform.

## Output Format

### BFD Profile: [Audience Name / Persona]

**Dominant Theme (one sentence):**
State the single most load-bearing insight. Write it as the reader would say it, not as a marketer would summarize it.

---

**Beliefs**
- [Belief 1 — specific, verbatim-adjacent]
- [Belief 2]
- [Belief 3]
- (3-5 beliefs; more is noise)

**Feelings**
- Primary feeling: [name it and describe what triggers it]
- Secondary feeling: [the one underneath]
- [Additional feeling if genuinely distinct]
- Relief portrait: [what "this worked" emotionally feels like for them]

**Desires**
- Surface desire: [the stated, functional outcome — what they say they want]
- Deep desire: [the identity / emotional transformation underneath — what they are actually buying]
- Success language: [how they would describe success in their own words]
- Failure fear: [what they are afraid of becoming if this doesn't work]

---

**BFD-to-Copy Bridge**

| Copy Element | BFD Source | Directive |
|---|---|---|
| Hook / Opening | Belief [X] | [specific instruction] |
| Tone | Feeling: [primary] | [specific instruction] |
| Headline | Deep desire | [specific instruction] |
| Objection handling | Belief [Y] | [specific instruction] |
| Testimonials | Deep desire | [specific instruction] |
| CTA | Deep desire | [specific instruction] |

---

**Research Gaps**
- [What is missing or assumed rather than sourced]
- [What a follow-up interview should probe]

## Anti-patterns

- **Assumption-based profiles** — writing a BFD brief from internal knowledge without a single primary source. This always produces the obvious angle and always underperforms.
- **Feature-level desires** — capturing "they want faster load times" as the desire instead of asking what faster load times actually give them. Surface desire ≠ deep desire.
- **Emotional generalism** — "they feel stressed" is not a feeling profile. Name the specific fear, the specific shame, the specific hope, and what triggers each one.
- **Belief inflation** — listing every belief from the research rather than identifying which two or three beliefs govern the rest. A brief with twelve beliefs is not a brief; it is a dump.
- **Skipping the dominant theme** — producing three populated buckets without synthesizing the one insight that governs all copy decisions. The dominant theme is the brief; the buckets are the evidence.
- **Conflating research and profile** — the profile is not a transcript of the research; it is a synthesis. Verbatim quotes are evidence to draw on, not content to paste in.
- **No bridge table** — delivering the BFD profile without mapping it to specific copy elements. The brief is not done until the copywriter knows which insight governs which decision.
- **Naming the deep desire too explicitly in copy** — telling the reader "you want to feel like a good person" triggers defensiveness. The deep desire belongs in the brief, not in the copy verbatim.

## Real examples

**Pet-relocation hero — PawRoute / Maya (expat leaving Dubai, dog is family, deepest fear is confiscation):**

- **Dominant theme:** "I need someone who has done this exact route before and will not let my dog be turned away at the border — I cannot afford a mistake."
- **Beliefs:** Pet relocation companies overpromise and underdeliver; the paperwork is impossible to do alone; government requirements change without warning and the companies don't track them.
- **Feelings:** Primary — fear of confiscation at the border (acute, visceral). Secondary — shame at not knowing what she doesn't know. Relief portrait — landing in the UK and seeing her dog walk out of customs healthy and documented, knowing she did it right.
- **Desires:** Surface — dog arrives safely. Deep — feel like a responsible pet owner who handled something genuinely complicated and got it right, without having to become an expert herself.
- **Copy output (headline):** "Get your dog from Dubai to the UK — without the confiscation risk."
- **Why it works:** Names the exact fear (confiscation), not the generic desire (safe arrival). Hits the deep desire obliquely — "without the confiscation risk" implies you handled the complexity she couldn't. The belief ("companies overpromise") is countered in the subheadline with specific proof: "DEFRA-compliant paperwork, IATA crate, named handler."

**IT professionals (Bly direct-mail example):**

- **Dominant theme (counterintuitive):** They want recognition, but they specifically do not want to deal with people to get it. The interpersonal friction of their role is the hidden frustration that the surface-desire ("career advancement") completely misses.
- **Surface desire:** Respect and career advancement.
- **Deep desire:** Appreciation for technical mastery that comes without having to manage relationships or navigate politics.
- **Copy impact:** The mailing that acknowledged the "no-people" dimension outperformed every version built on the standard professional-advancement angle.
- **Lesson:** The counterintuitive BFD finding — the one that contradicts the obvious assumption — is where the highest-converting copy lives.

## Self-check validation

Before delivering a BFD profile, confirm:

- [ ] **Dominant theme stated** — one sentence, reader-language, not marketer-language.
- [ ] **Beliefs are specific** — each belief is traceable to a research source, not assumed; 3-5 beliefs, not a kitchen sink.
- [ ] **Feelings are named precisely** — primary and secondary feelings identified; "stressed" is not a feeling name; "fear of being judged as negligent" is.
- [ ] **Surface vs. deep desire separated** — both named; the deep desire passes the three-times-why test.
- [ ] **Research sources cited** — the brief notes which findings come from interviews, reviews, forums, or assumption.
- [ ] **Bridge table complete** — every major copy element has a BFD source and a directive.
- [ ] **Research gaps flagged** — the brief is honest about what is assumed vs. evidenced.
- [ ] **No belief inflation** — fewer than six beliefs; if more than five, consolidate.
- [ ] **Deep desire is not in the copy verbatim** — the brief captures it; the copy names it obliquely.

## Known gaps

- **Does not conduct the research** — this skill synthesises and profiles; it does not run interviews, mine reviews, or set up research projects. Use **customer-research** for the full research-gathering workflow.
- **Persona ≠ segment** — a BFD profile is written for a single, specific reader archetype. If the audience has multiple distinct segments, run a separate profile for each; blending them produces a profile that speaks to no one.
- **No quantitative validation** — BFD profiles are qualitative by nature. Hypotheses about which belief or desire is dominant must be tested in copy; use **ab-testing** to validate which BFD angle converts.
- **Deep desire is hard to name** — the three-times-why technique is reliable but not infallible. When the deep desire is still unclear after the exercise, look at the inverse: what does the reader fear becoming if they fail? That failure-identity is the photographic negative of the deep desire.
- **Research quality varies** — profiles built on competitor reviews are noisier than profiles built on direct interviews. The brief should note its source quality; a copywriter working from a review-only profile should treat it as a first hypothesis, not a settled brief.
