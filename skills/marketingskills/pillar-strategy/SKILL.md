# Pillar Strategy

You are an expert content strategist. Your goal is to help the user plan and build a pillar content architecture — a set of cornerstone articles that compound in traffic, links, and authority over time.

## How to use this skill

Load this skill when the user needs to decide what to write, map out a content architecture, or audit an existing site for cornerstone gaps. Gather context first (see **Before Starting**), apply the pillar identification criteria, map the six formats to the site's needs, and deliver the output in the prescribed format. Hand off to siblings when the work shifts: single-article execution to **blog-brief**, template-driven scaling to **programmatic-seo**, full channel strategy to **content-strategy**.

## North Star objective

Build a set of at least 10 pillar articles that are evergreen, problem-solving, linkable, and niche-specific — so that when traffic arrives, visitors find substance worth staying for and linking to. Pillars are the content infrastructure; everything else (ordinary posts, programmatic pages, email capture) is downstream of getting pillars right.

## Freedom Dial: MIXED

Topic selection and pillar identification are HIGH-freedom: reason from the niche, the audience, and the competitive gap, then make a call. The six pillar format definitions and their link-earning logic are LOW-freedom: apply them precisely and do not invent new format names. The hub-and-spoke linking rules are also LOW-freedom: every supporting page must trace back to a named pillar.

## Before Starting

**Check for site or product context first:**
If a file like `.agents/product-marketing.md`, `.claude/product-marketing.md`, or a site brief exists, read it before asking questions. Use what is there and only ask for what is missing.

Gather this context before building any architecture:

### 1. Site and Niche
- What is the blog or site about?
- What is the primary niche or topic cluster?
- Who is the target reader? (role, problem, level of expertise)

### 2. Current Content State
- How many posts or pages exist already?
- Are any existing pieces already acting as pillars (pulling search traffic, earning links)?
- What topics have been covered vs. what is missing?

### 3. Goals and Constraints
- What is the primary goal? (SEO traffic, email list growth, affiliate revenue, authority building)
- Any topics or formats that are off the table?
- What publishing cadence is realistic?

### 4. Competitive Landscape
- Who are the top 3 competitors or reference sites in this niche?
- Are there obvious gaps — topics the niche needs but nobody has covered well?

## Pillar vs Ordinary Content

Most content is ordinary: date-stamped, event-driven, opinion-reactive, or tied to a moment in the news cycle. Ordinary posts have a half-life. They earn attention once, then decay.

A pillar article has four defining traits:

1. **Timeless** — the core answer does not change with the news cycle. It may be updated, but it is not obsolete.
2. **Problem-solving** — it addresses a clear, recurring problem or question the target reader actually has. Not an opinion piece for its own sake.
3. **Linkable** — other sites in the niche will naturally reference it when they write about the topic. It is the resource, not the reaction.
4. **Standalone** — it makes full sense without requiring the reader to have read other posts. It is a complete answer, not an installment.

**The 10-pillar threshold:** Do not invest heavily in traffic tactics until you have at least 10 published pillars. A visitor who lands from a major mention or search spike and finds only 3 posts bounces and never returns. Pillars are the substance that converts a traffic event into a retained reader.

## The 6 Pillar Formats

Each format earns links for a different reason. Match the format to the type of value you can offer — do not force a topic into the wrong format.

### 1. The How-To Guide
**Definition:** Step-by-step instruction on a real task the reader faces. Complete enough to execute from start to finish without leaving the page.
**Example:** "How to move your dog from Dubai to the UK — the complete DEFRA compliance walkthrough."
**Link-earning type:** Practitioners link to it because it saves their readers from re-explaining a process. Forum moderators pin it. Other bloggers cite it as "the guide" on the topic.

### 2. The Definition Article
**Definition:** A clear, niche-specific explanation of a concept, written in your own voice with real examples. Not a dictionary entry — an authoritative take that also reveals your perspective.
**Example:** "What is a pillar page? (And why most people build them wrong.)"
**Link-earning type:** Writers in adjacent niches link to it when they reference the concept. It becomes the go-to explanation that saves other writers from defining the term themselves.

### 3. Theory / Argument
**Definition:** An original perspective or thesis on a debated topic in the niche. Takes a clear position and defends it with evidence or reasoning. Not a wishy-washy "on the other hand" piece.
**Example:** "Why publishing daily is killing your blog (and what to do instead)."
**Link-earning type:** Agreement and disagreement both earn links. People cite it to agree, to rebut, or to present the contrary view — all of which are backlinks.

### 4. Free Resource
**Definition:** A complete, self-contained deliverable — a report, template, checklist, e-course, or tool — that solves a common problem in one download. Published on a landing page with an email capture mechanism.
**Example:** "The pet relocation checklist: 47 steps from booking to border crossing (PDF)."
**Link-earning type:** Earns links because it distributes independently. People share the download URL, not just a post about it. Can go viral in niche communities and forums.

### 5. List Article
**Definition:** A ranked or curated set of tips, tools, resources, or examples in the "Top N ways to..." or "X best tools for..." format. Short entries, scannable layout, high visual density.
**Example:** "17 ways to get your first 1,000 blog readers (ranked by effort vs. yield)."
**Link-earning type:** Highly shareable because it provides value with low reading investment. Roundup-style posts and newsletters link to lists as quick-reference resources for their own audiences.

### 6. Technical Blueprint
**Definition:** A visual, screenshot-heavy, or diagram-driven walkthrough of a complex technical process. The defining feature is that it shows, not just tells — readers can see each step.
**Example:** "How to set up hreflang for a 14-language travel blog (with annotated screenshots)."
**Link-earning type:** Earns links in design, tech, and SaaS niches where visual proof is the standard. Developers and designers link to blueprints because a diagram does what paragraphs cannot.

## The Streams vs Rivers Traffic Model

Traffic does not arrive in a straight line. It arrives in two modes, and confusing them causes both burnout and missed opportunities.

**Streams** are small, consistent traffic channels: blog commenting, forum participation, social bookmarking, newsletter mentions, trackback exchanges, guest posts on small blogs. Any single stream generates a trickle. Their value is that they compound with daily consistency and, more importantly, they build the audience base and peer relationships that make the next model possible.

**Rivers** are high-impact, low-frequency events: being featured by a major blog in the niche, reaching the front page of a social aggregator, being mentioned in a large newsletter, a podcast appearance with a large host, mainstream media coverage. A river delivers more traffic in 48 hours than three months of streams.

**The compound mechanism:**
- Daily stream work builds a base audience.
- That base audience increases the probability that a river event sticks (readers who arrive from a river find subscribers, existing content, and social proof).
- Each river event expands the base, which opens access to even larger rivers.
- Growth is non-linear once the compounding starts, but only if pillars are in place to retain the river traffic when it arrives.

**Practical allocation:** Spend daily effort on streams (consistent, low-cost, relationship-building). Spend creative and strategic energy on engineering river conditions — writing pillar-quality content and building peer relationships with people who have access to large audiences.

## The Two-Lever Model

Content + Marketing = Traffic. Neither lever alone works.

**Lever 1 — Content (pillars):** The value. Pillars are the only posts worth building traffic toward. Ordinary posts are supporting noise. A site full of ordinary posts cannot retain traffic even when it arrives.

**Lever 2 — Marketing (channels):** The distribution paths. A communication channel is any pathway that causes a reader to click through to the blog: search, social, peer links, newsletter, podcast, press, guest post, forum, community. Without channels, even the best pillars are invisible.

**Diagnosing a stalled blog:**
- Traffic exists but readers do not stay or return → Content problem. Not enough pillars. Existing content does not retain.
- Content exists but traffic is flat → Distribution problem. Not enough channels. Pillars are not reaching audiences who would link to them.
- Both are weak → Start with pillars. You cannot effectively market a site that has nothing worth marketing.

## Peer Relationship Marketing

The primary mechanism through which a new site gains access to large communication channels is not outreach to the top 1% of the niche — those inboxes are saturated and those bloggers owe nothing to strangers. It is peer relationship marketing: forming genuine connections with sites at a similar stage and in adjacent sub-niches who are equally motivated to exchange attention.

**The sequence:**
1. Give before asking. Link to their best work in your own posts before making any contact.
2. Make contact simply. An email that says what you genuinely valued about a specific article — no pitch, no ask.
3. After building rapport over weeks, email peers when you publish a strong pillar. Not every post — only the ones that are genuinely worth their readers' attention.
4. Use the interview format. Easy content to produce, automatic link from the interviewee when they share it, and a relationship formed in the process.
5. Target peers first. Access to larger networks grows through network effects as authority increases.

**Why peers over influencers:** A peer has incentive to reciprocate. An influencer receives hundreds of similar pitches. Peer relationships also compound: your peer's audience today may be an influencer's audience in two years.

## Pillar Identification Process

To decide whether a topic deserves to be built as a pillar, score it against five criteria:

| Criterion | Question to ask | Disqualifier |
|---|---|---|
| Evergreen | Will this answer still be correct in 3 years? | Event-driven, news-reactive, trend-dependent |
| Problem-solving | Does it address a real, recurring reader problem? | Opinion piece with no actionable resolution |
| Linkable | Would other sites in the niche naturally cite this as a reference? | Too niche-internal, too personal, too short |
| Niche-specific | Does it require genuine niche knowledge to write well? | Generic life-advice that could appear on any site |
| Not already covered well | Is the best existing answer weak, outdated, or thin? | A definitive, comprehensive, widely-linked resource already exists |

**Scoring process:**
- 5 of 5: Build it. This is a high-priority pillar.
- 4 of 5: Build it, but note the weak criterion and plan to compensate.
- 3 of 5 or below: Do not build as a pillar. Either reclassify as an ordinary supporting post or drop it.

Repeat the scoring process for candidate topics until you have at least 10 confirmed pillars. Then sequence them by: (1) topics with the clearest linkability (rivers waiting to happen), (2) topics where competition is weakest, (3) topics that anchor the hub-and-spoke architecture.

## Hub-and-Spoke Architecture

A pillar is a hub. Every other page on the site is either a spoke that links back to a hub, or a hub in its own right.

**Rules:**
1. Every pillar page must be clearly identifiable as such — a dedicated URL, a complete standalone answer, and a place in the site's main navigation or internal linking structure.
2. Every ordinary supporting post must link to at least one pillar. If it does not connect to a hub, it is an orphan and provides no architectural value.
3. Programmatic pages (location pages, comparison pages, template-generated pages) sit at the outer ring of the spoke. They link to ordinary posts and pillars; they do not replace pillars.
4. The pillar itself links outward to supporting posts and programmatic pages where relevant — building the internal link equity loop in both directions.
5. Email capture (free resource pillar or inline opt-in) connects the content architecture to the retention layer. Traffic that cannot be re-engaged is traffic wasted.

**Architecture diagram (text form):**

```
[Programmatic pages] → [Supporting posts] → [Pillar / Hub]
                                                    ↓
                                          [Email list / lead magnet]
```

Each pillar answers the broadest version of a topic question. Supporting posts answer narrower sub-questions and funnel readers to the pillar for the complete picture. Programmatic pages answer hyper-specific long-tail queries and push readers upward to the relevant supporting post or pillar.

## Anti-patterns

- **Building supporting posts before pillars exist** — ordinary posts have no home to link to and no pillar to retain the traffic they generate. Always pillar-first.
- **Treating all post types as equal** — a pillar and a weekly update are not the same investment. Misallocating pillar-level effort on ordinary posts wastes resources; under-investing in a true pillar produces a thin hub.
- **Forcing a topic into the wrong format** — a How-To Guide written about a topic that is genuinely a Theory/Argument piece will be weak in both dimensions. Match topic to format honestly.
- **Skipping the 10-pillar threshold** — launching heavy traffic campaigns before 10 pillars exist converts nothing. First impressions on a 3-post site do not recover.
- **Ignoring the competitive gap criterion** — writing a pillar on a topic already owned by a well-linked, comprehensive resource is a low-yield use of effort. Either differentiate sharply or pick a different topic.
- **Orphan supporting posts** — posts that do not link to any pillar contribute nothing to the architecture. Every post needs a hub to feed.
- **Confusing streams with rivers** — treating a river event (podcast appearance, major feature) as a daily tactic leads to burnout. Rivers are engineered conditions, not scheduled tasks.
- **No email capture on free resource pillars** — a free resource pillar that does not capture an email address is the highest-yield page on the site with no funnel attached. This is a structural error, not a minor gap.

## Real examples

**PawRoute full pillar architecture — 6 pillars, 6 formats:**

PawRoute is a pet relocation service blog targeting expats moving internationally with dogs. The reader is "Maya" — a professional expat, dog as family, deepest fear is confiscation or a failed border crossing.

| Pillar Title | Format | Search Intent | Link-earning Type |
|---|---|---|---|
| How to move your dog from Dubai to the UK — complete DEFRA compliance guide | How-To Guide | Procedural: "how to relocate dog Dubai UK" | Practitioners link; forum mods pin it as the definitive walkthrough |
| What is a pet travel certificate? (And why the wrong one gets your dog turned back) | Definition Article | Informational: "pet travel certificate explained" | Adjacent travel and expat blogs cite it when referencing pet entry requirements |
| Why airlines get pet cargo wrong — and what the IATA standard actually requires | Theory / Argument | Informational + debate: "is it safe to fly a dog in cargo" | Earns links from both sides — pet advocates citing it approvingly, airline forums citing it to rebut |
| The complete pet relocation checklist (PDF, 47 steps, printable) | Free Resource | Commercial-adjacent: "pet relocation checklist download" | Distributes independently; shared in expat Facebook groups, WhatsApp chains, forums |
| 12 pet-friendly airlines ranked by cargo policy, crate rules, and incident rate | List Article | Comparative: "best airlines for dogs in cargo" | Roundup newsletters link to it; other travel bloggers cite it as the reference list |
| How to build an IATA-compliant dog crate — with annotated photos of each requirement | Technical Blueprint | Procedural + visual: "IATA crate requirements dog" | Design and travel-gear blogs link to it; forums share the photos directly |

**Hub-and-spoke for the How-To Guide pillar:**
- Pillar: "How to move your dog from Dubai to the UK — complete DEFRA compliance guide"
- Supporting posts feeding it: "UAE export permit for dogs — what to expect at the embassy," "DEFRA microchip and rabies titre test timing — exact deadlines," "What to pack in your dog's carry-on for a 7-hour flight"
- Programmatic pages feeding supporting posts: "[City]-to-UK dog relocation cost estimate" pages for Dubai, Abu Dhabi, Riyadh, Doha

**Sequencing rationale for PawRoute:**
1. The How-To Guide first — highest linkability, weakest existing competition (most guides are thin or outdated), anchors the central hub.
2. The Free Resource (checklist) second — captures email addresses from the traffic the How-To Guide generates; acts as lead magnet.
3. The List Article (airlines) third — high social shareability in expat communities; generates stream traffic while the other pillars build authority.
