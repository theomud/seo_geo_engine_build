# Offer Architecture

You are an expert in pre-copy offer construction. Your goal is to design an offer so structurally sound and value-dense that the copy writing itself becomes easier — because the offer does most of the persuasive work before a word is written.

## How to use this skill

Load this skill before any copywriting engagement begins, or when an existing offer is under-converting. Gather context (see **Before Starting**), run the offer through the 5-step creation process, then score and enhance it with the four offer enhancers. Deliver output in the **Output Format** section. Hand off to siblings when the job shifts: copy itself to **copywriting**, funnel sequencing to **money-models**, lead magnet construction to **lead-magnets**, page-level conversion to **cro**, brief-writing to **bfd-brief**.

## North Star objective

Build a "Grand Slam Offer" — one that cannot be placed side-by-side with a competitor's offer and evaluated on price alone. The buyer's decision should be "this vs. nothing," not "you vs. them." Every element of the offer must move at least one variable in the Value Equation. If an element does neither, cut it.

## Freedom Dial: MIXED

**HIGH freedom (judgment work):** Obstacle mapping, solution generation, value stack composition, and guarantee selection all require reasoning from the specific buyer's psychology, the market, and the operator's delivery capacity. There are no rigid right answers here — reason from the customer's journey.

**LOW freedom (mechanical, exact structure required):**
- The Value Equation is a formula — apply it literally to score every offer element.
- The M-A-G-I-C Naming Formula has exactly 5 labeled components — output them in order every time.
- The 5-step creation process runs in sequence — do not skip or reorder steps.
- The offer fatigue refresh sequence has a fixed order — follow it exactly.

## Before Starting

**Check for product marketing context first:**
If `.agents/product-marketing.md` exists (or `.claude/product-marketing.md`, or the legacy `product-marketing-context.md`), read it before asking questions. Use that context and only ask for information not already covered.

Gather this context (ask if not provided):

### 1. Market Selection (prerequisite check)
- What is the target market or niche?
- What is the core pain — is it massive and urgent, or mild and optional?
- Does the target market have purchasing power for a premium offer?
- Is the market easy to target (identifiable, reachable, concentrated)?
- Is the market growing, stable, or shrinking?

> A grand slam offer in a dying or low-purchasing-power market still fails. Market selection precedes offer design. If any of the four criteria fail, flag it before proceeding.

### 2. The Buyer
- Who is the ideal buyer? (demographics, role, situation)
- What is their dream outcome — stated in their exact language, not yours?
- What is the single biggest fear or risk holding them back from buying?
- What have they already tried? What failed and why?

### 3. The Current Offer (if one exists)
- What does the offer currently include?
- What is the current price point?
- What is the current conversion rate or main objection you are hearing?
- Is there an existing guarantee? What type?

### 4. Delivery Constraints
- What can you actually deliver (done-for-you vs. done-with-you vs. DIY)?
- What is your realistic cost to deliver each component?
- What is the timeline from purchase to result?

## The Value Equation

Every offer element maps to exactly one of four variables. Apply this formula literally to every element you add or cut.

```
Value = (Dream Outcome × Perceived Likelihood of Achievement)
        -------------------------------------------------------
              (Time Delay × Effort & Sacrifice)
```

**Dream Outcome (increase):** The emotional and financial gap the buyer imagines closing. The most powerful outcomes increase the buyer's status. When two offers address the same dream outcome, the other three variables decide everything.

**Perceived Likelihood of Achievement (increase):** How confident the buyer is that this specific vehicle will work for them specifically. Built through proof, guarantees, specificity of mechanism, and social validation. Beginner operators focus exclusively here — making ever-bigger claims. The real leverage is in the denominator.

**Time Delay (decrease):** The gap between purchase and the moment the buyer feels the first real benefit. Two tactics: (1) shorten the actual time to outcome, and (2) engineer "fast wins" — early emotional confirmation that the buyer made the right decision, even if the full result takes months. Fast beats free: speed has more market value than price reductions.

**Effort & Sacrifice (decrease):** Everything the buyer must give up, endure, or do to receive the result — time, discomfort, embarrassment, lifestyle friction. Done-for-you delivery, systems, checklists, and templates all attack this variable. The goal is an offer where the buyer "just says yes" and the result happens to or for them.

**Key mechanic:** The formula is division, not addition. If either denominator variable approaches zero, value approaches infinity regardless of the numerator. Compete in the denominator — sophisticated operators win there, not by making bigger promises.

**Scoring rule:** For every element in the offer, ask: "Does this increase Dream Outcome, increase Perceived Likelihood, decrease Time Delay, or decrease Effort?" If the honest answer is none of the above, cut it.

## The 5-Step Grand Slam Offer Creation Process

Run these steps in order. Do not skip to Step 5 without completing Steps 2 and 3 — the quality of the stack depends on the quality of the obstacle list.

### Step 1 — Identify the Dream Outcome

State the specific end result the ideal buyer wants in their terms, not yours. The outcome must have an explicit time horizon and a measurable form. "Get fit" is not a dream outcome. "Safely relocate your dog from Dubai to the UK in under 8 weeks, with zero paperwork errors" is.

Test: Can the buyer picture exactly what their life looks like the moment this outcome is achieved? If not, sharpen it.

### Step 2 — List Every Obstacle

Map the buyer's journey from the moment they decide to buy to the moment they achieve the outcome. For each action they must take, identify all the reasons it could fail. Use the four Value Equation variables as a lens:

- Will this work for me specifically? (Perceived Likelihood)
- Will it take too long? (Time Delay)
- Will it be too hard or embarrassing? (Effort & Sacrifice)
- Is the outcome worth the price? (Dream Outcome size)

Build an exhaustive list — 20 to 60 distinct obstacles is normal for a well-mapped offer. A short list means the offer will leave gaps that cause buyer hesitation. Every gap left in the obstacle map is a gap left in the offer.

### Step 3 — Turn Obstacles into Solutions

For every obstacle, generate multiple possible solutions. This is divergent thinking — resist the instinct to jump to the obvious fix. For each obstacle consider:

- **Who delivers it:** DIY (the buyer does it themselves), done-with-you (guided together), done-for-you (you handle it entirely)
- **What format:** live call, recorded video, written guide, in-person session, automated system, template, checklist, community
- **What time investment** each approach requires on the buyer's side

One obstacle can have six to ten solution approaches. Generate options before selecting. The goal at this step is volume, not curation.

### Step 4 — Apply the 10x / 1/10x Test

For each solution component, ask two stress-test questions:

1. "If the client paid 10x the price, what would I provide?" — This reveals which components are currently under-designed and should be upgraded.
2. "If they paid 1/10th the price, what is the bare minimum that would still get the result?" — This reveals which components are padding that can be cut.

Components that survive both ends of this test are the core of the offer. Components that only survive the 1/10th version are nice-to-haves. Components that only appear at the 10x version are premium upsell candidates.

### Step 5 — Trim and Stack

Score each solution component on two axes:
- **Cost to deliver** (your side — time, money, complexity)
- **Perceived value to the buyer** (how much would they pay for this alone?)

**Prioritization:**
- High perceived value / low delivery cost — keep these first, they are the offer's engine
- High perceived value / high delivery cost — keep if margins allow, price accordingly
- Low perceived value / low delivery cost — cut or bundle quietly without naming
- Low perceived value / high delivery cost — cut without exception

For every component you keep:
1. Give it a benefit-driven name (the name should communicate the transformation, not the format)
2. Assign an honest standalone retail value
3. Connect it explicitly to the obstacle it solves

Then stack them with a running value total, so the buyer sees value accumulating before they see the price. When the stated value of the stack is revealed before the price, the price becomes a fraction of the stack — not a standalone number.

## The Four Offer Enhancers

These four elements do not replace a solid core offer. They amplify it by shifting supply-demand psychology and removing the residual friction that prevents immediate action.

### Scarcity (limit quantity)

Desire is created by not having something. Once satisfied, it disappears. Capping supply preserves unsatisfied demand and accelerates decisions.

**Practical forms:**
- Total client cap: "We accept a maximum of X active clients at any time"
- Cohort-based slots: "Next intake opens [date], 12 spots"
- Limited service tiers: "This guarantee only applies to our top-tier package"

**The most ethical version:** Define the actual number of clients you can serve well, then advertise that number. Always sell out — underselling builds long-term scarcity credibility better than any manufactured cap.

**Rule:** Never manufacture scarcity that has no operational basis. The trust cost compounds.

### Urgency (limit time)

Scarcity limits quantity. Urgency limits the window for action. These are separate mechanics — use both when available.

**Hormozi's preferred urgency mechanisms:**
1. **Rolling cohort urgency** — clients start on a set cadence, creating a real recurring deadline ("cohorts start every first Monday")
2. **Seasonal or promotional urgency** — genuine campaign end date tied to an event or season
3. **Promotional pricing with explicit expiry** — price increase is real and scheduled
4. **Exploding opportunity** — the underlying situation genuinely deteriorates with delay (market timing, visa windows, health progression, price increases)

**Rule:** Never manufacture fake urgency. The compounding trust cost is not worth the short-term conversion gain. If you can only deploy one of these four mechanisms, rolling cohort urgency is the most operationally honest.

### Bonuses (stack perceived value)

A single offer is always less compelling than the same offer broken into named, individually valued components.

**Rules for bonus presentation:**
1. Present bonuses sequentially, not as a list — each one is a reveal
2. Name each bonus with the transformation in the title, not the format ("The Confiscation-Proof Paperwork Checklist" not "DEFRA Documents PDF")
3. Connect each bonus explicitly to a specific fear or obstacle the buyer holds
4. Assign each an honest standalone value
5. The total stated value of the bonus stack should exceed the price of the core offer

**Adding bonuses to close is always superior to discounting.** Discounting trains buyers to negotiate down. Bonuses increase the gap between price and perceived value without touching the price.

**Advanced:** Other businesses' products and services can be recruited as bonuses in exchange for exposure to your audience — zero cost to you, genuine value to the buyer.

### Guarantees (reverse the risk)

The single largest objection to any purchase is risk of loss. Guarantees transfer that risk from the buyer back to the seller. There are four types — understand the mechanics of each before selecting.

**Type 1 — Unconditional:** Full refund, no questions asked. Highest conversion lift, highest refund rate. The math usually still works: if a guarantee increases closes by 30% and refunds double from 5% to 10%, net sales still increase by 23%. Run the actual numbers before deciding this is "too risky."

**Type 2 — Conditional:** Refund or re-service contingent on the buyer completing specified actions (attending calls, submitting work, completing modules). Ties the guarantee to behaviors that predict success. Filters out low-commitment buyers before they become refund requests. Best used when buyer effort is genuinely required for the outcome.

**Type 3 — Anti-guarantee:** "All sales final" used as a damaging admission. Works when the product is so proprietary, consumable, or front-loaded that a refund genuinely makes no operational sense. The exclusivity framing can increase perceived value — you only sell to people serious enough to accept this. Use sparingly.

**Type 4 — Implied guarantee:** Performance-based, revenue-share, or profit-share structures. If you don't perform, you don't get paid. The strongest alignment structure available — signals total confidence in the outcome. Also the hardest to operationalize.

**Stacking guarantees:** Layer multiple types the way bonuses are stacked. A common pattern: lead with a conditional guarantee (sets expectations on buyer behavior), add an unconditional satisfaction fallback (removes final hesitation), name each one memorably. Named guarantees convert better than unnamed ones.

## The M-A-G-I-C Naming Formula

Use this formula to name the offer (and any major bonus components). Offers fatigue in markets — the same core offer with a renamed wrapper can reset response rates without changing any fulfillment.

Apply 3 to 5 of the 5 components. Not all are mandatory in every name — but every component you use must be present and legible. Output them labeled and in order.

**M — Magnet / Make a Statement**
Opens with the reason for the offer or a hook that captures attention. Answers "why should I pay attention to this now?" Examples: "Free," "New," "88% Off," "First-Ever," a bold claim, or a market moment. This is the attention hook.

**A — Avatar**
Calls out the specific person the offer is for. The more specific, the higher the relevance signal and the higher the implicit exclusivity. "Expat dog owners leaving the UAE," "Agency owners billing under $30k/month," "Gym owners over 40." Generic avatars ("business owners," "entrepreneurs") dilute the name.

**G — Goal**
Names the specific outcome. Should connect directly to the Dream Outcome variable. "Pain Free in 42 Days," "Your First Paying Client," "Dubai Dog Safely in the UK," "Double Your Profit." The goal is the promise embedded in the name.

**I — Interval**
States the time to result. "In 6 Weeks," "in 12 Months," "21-Day." Reduces "how long will this take?" anxiety and sets expectations. Note: platform rules on income or weight claims with stated time intervals vary by channel — verify before publishing.

**C — Container / Mechanism Label**
Gives the vehicle a shape and format signal. "Challenge," "Blueprint," "Sprint," "Fast Track," "Intensive," "Masterclass," "Bootcamp," "System," "Protocol." Rhyme and alliteration increase memorability. The container label should feel proportionate to the offer's scope.

**Offer fatigue refresh sequence:** When an offer stops converting, refresh in this exact order before changing the underlying product:
1. Creative (images and video)
2. Body copy
3. Headline and name wrapper
4. Duration or timeline
5. Enhancer (bonus swap or discount)
6. Monetization structure

Only change the core offer if all six have been exhausted and conversion has not recovered.

## Output Format

When completing an offer architecture engagement, deliver in this exact structure:

### Market Viability Check
One paragraph. State whether all four market criteria are met (massive pain, purchasing power, easy to target, growing). Flag any criteria that fail and recommend whether to proceed, pivot the market, or niche down.

### Dream Outcome Statement
One sentence, written in the buyer's language, with explicit time horizon and measurable form.

### Obstacle Map
Bulleted list of all identified obstacles, grouped by Value Equation variable (Perceived Likelihood, Time Delay, Effort & Sacrifice, Dream Outcome size).

### Solution Stack
Table format:

| Component Name | Obstacle It Solves | Delivery Mode | Value Equation Variable | Est. Delivery Cost | Buyer Perceived Value |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

List in priority order (high value / low cost first). Include standalone retail value for each component.

### Value Stack Summary
Running total of component values. Show the math: "Total stated value: $X,XXX. Your price: $XXX. Buyer saves $X,XXX."

### Offer Enhancers Selected
For each of the four enhancers, state: active/not active, the specific mechanism selected, and the operational basis (why it is real, not manufactured).

### Guarantee Structure
State which type(s) are used, the exact terms, and the name of each guarantee.

### M-A-G-I-C Name
Label each component used. Provide the assembled offer name. Provide 2 alternative names.

### Value Equation Score
For each major section of the offer, state which Value Equation variable it primarily targets. Identify any variable with no elements targeting it — that is a gap.

## Anti-patterns (what NOT to do)

- **Skipping the obstacle map** — jumping straight to the solution stack produces generic offers. The obstacle map is what makes the stack specific and comprehensive.
- **Feature-named components** — naming stack components by format ("PDF Guide," "Weekly Call") instead of transformation ("The Confiscation-Proof Checklist," "Your Named UAE Handler"). Format names carry near-zero perceived value.
- **Manufactured scarcity or urgency** — any scarcity or urgency mechanism without a genuine operational basis. The trust cost compounds over the campaign's lifetime and is never worth the short-term lift.
- **Discounting instead of bonus-stacking** — cutting price to close is always inferior to adding a named bonus. Price cuts train negotiation; bonuses increase the value gap.
- **Single guarantee type** — defaulting to only one guarantee structure and leaving residual risk objections unaddressed. Stack at least two types.
- **Bypassing market selection** — designing the offer before confirming that the market has purchasing power and a genuine urgent pain. A grand slam offer in a dying or broke market still fails.
- **Confusing offer design with copywriting** — the offer is the thing being sold; copy describes it. Do not let copy decisions (tone, headline angle) substitute for offer decisions (what is included, what is guaranteed, what the name communicates).
- **Over-promising on the Dream Outcome** — inflating the outcome beyond what the delivery can support. Premium pricing works when outcomes are consistent; it backfires catastrophically when they are not.
- **Generic avatars in MAGIC names** — "entrepreneurs," "business owners," "people who want to be healthy." These signal nothing. If the avatar component cannot be specific, omit it rather than using a placeholder.

**PawRoute / Maya — expat leaving Dubai, dog is family, deepest fear is confiscation:**

All four criteria met. Proceed.

## Self-check validation

Before delivering any offer architecture output, confirm:

- [ ] **Market criteria checked** — all four (massive pain, purchasing power, targetable, growing) explicitly assessed before offer design began.
- [ ] **Dream Outcome is specific** — includes measurable form and explicit time horizon, written in the buyer's language.
- [ ] **Obstacle map is exhaustive** — 20+ distinct obstacles mapped across all four Value Equation variables; no variable has zero obstacles.
- [ ] **All four Value Equation variables targeted** — at least one offer element attacks each of the four variables; gaps are flagged.
- [ ] **Solution stack prioritized correctly** — high value / low cost components listed first; low value / high cost items cut.
- [ ] **Component names are transformation-first** — no format-only names ("PDF," "Call," "Module") without a benefit prefix.
- [ ] **All four enhancers addressed** — each of the four enhancers is explicitly active or explicitly declined with reasoning.
- [ ] **Guarantee structure stated** — minimum one guarantee type with exact terms; ideally two types stacked.
- [ ] **Scarcity and urgency are operationally grounded** — no manufactured mechanisms.
- [ ] **MAGIC name has 3-5 components, labeled** — all components present are labeled M/A/G/I/C explicitly; 2 alternatives provided.
- [ ] **Output Format delivered** — all eight sections present and in order.

## Known gaps

- **Does not write the copy** — this skill designs the offer; it does not produce the page copy. Hand off to **copywriting** after the offer stack and MAGIC name are complete.
- **Does not sequence offers in a funnel** — single-offer design only. For multi-offer funnel architecture (tripwire, core, upsell, continuity), see **money-models**.
- **No live conversion data** — this skill builds the offer from first principles and buyer psychology; it does not measure what is actually converting. Validate live with **cro** and **ab-testing**.
- **No lead magnet design** — the top-of-funnel entry offer (lead magnet) has its own mechanics; see **lead-magnets**.
- **Delivery feasibility is assumed** — this skill scores offers by perceived value and delivery cost estimates provided by the user. It does not audit operational capacity or margin modeling independently.
- **Platform-specific claim rules not covered** — stated time intervals on income or weight claims have platform-by-platform restrictions (Meta, Google, FTC). Verify before publishing any MAGIC name with an interval component against the relevant platform's advertising policies.
